## 1. Does a Niuean word-frequency list or corpus exist? If not, what proxies work?

### Takeaway
I found no modern, machine-readable Niuean frequency list or corpus. One historical frequency list does exist: Elley & Livingstone's 1970s NZCER spelling list, built from Niuean children's compositions. Its 900 words reportedly covered 95% of all running words. That figure suggests a ~1,000–1,500-word core is a realistic target for everyday Niuean. The best practical proxies are the Niuean Bible (1904 and 1983 editions) and Sperlich's 1997 dictionary (partly searchable through Pollex). A translated general core list, checked against curriculum and community materials, would also work.

### Cited Findings
- **Elley & Livingstone word list (NZCER).** In 1973 the Niue government asked the New Zealand Council for Educational Research for a spelling word list for Niuean schools. The resulting list has **900 words, each occurring at least 10 times in children's written compositions**. Together these 900 words account for **95% of all running words**. The compositions contained about **4,500 different words** in total. The list was ranked by absolute frequency, which proved more stable than counting how many essays a word appeared in. — [Commonwealth iLibrary PDF (search snippet)](https://thecommonwealth-ilibrary.org/index.php/comsec/catalog/download/247/244/1791?inline=1)
- Compared with a New Zealand English spelling list, Niuean children used words like coconut, plantation, village and island much more often. The authors concluded that Niuean schools needed their own list rather than a translated English one. — [same source](https://thecommonwealth-ilibrary.org/index.php/comsec/catalog/download/247/244/1791?inline=1)
- **Bible text.** Find.bible lists two Niuean Bibles: a 1983 Niue Bible Society edition and the 1904 *Holy Bible in Niue*. The language code is `niu`. — [find.bible](https://find.bible/en/languages/niu)
- The OPUS "bible-uedin" parallel corpus (Christodoulopoulos & Steedman, ~100 Bible translations) exists, but no snippet confirmed that Niuean is included. — [OPUS bible-uedin](https://opus.nlpl.eu/bible-uedin/corpus/version/bible-uedin); [Christodoulopoulos & Steedman paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10579-014-9287-y)
- A secondary source says the spelling of the Niuean Bible serves as a reference point for orthography. — [Wikipedia mirror, ISO 639:niu (snippet)](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/ISO_639:niu)
- **Dictionaries (no frequency data).** Pollex-Online, hosted by the Max Planck Institute, includes Niuean entries from McEwen's 1970 *Niue Dictionary* and Sperlich's 1997 *Niue Language Dictionary*. Sperlich's dictionary was published by the Government of Niue with the University of Hawaiʻi Department of Linguistics. — [Pollex: Sperlich 1997](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/source/219/); [Pollex: McEwen 1970](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/source/147/?page=9)
- **Swadesh-type lists.** ASJP has a short basic-vocabulary list for Niuean (about 40 items, the ASJP standard). — [ASJP: Niue](https://asjp.clld.org/languages/NIUE)
- **NZ education resources.** The Ministry for Pacific Peoples publishes Niuean orthography guidelines. Te Kete Ipurangi (TKI) hosts *Ponataki*, a Niue language educational resource. — [MPP orthography guidelines (Niue)](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Resources/Orthography-guidelines/orthography-guidelines-niue.pdf); [Ponataki (TKI)](https://pasifika.tki.org.nz/content/download/2963/21181/file/Ponataki+-+Niue+Language+Educational+Resource.pdf)
- The University of Auckland publishes a 2023 Niuean course and information sheet. — [UoA niuean-2023.pdf](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/assets/science/for/maori-and-pacific-students/pacific-languages/niuean-2023.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Elley & Livingstone result (900 words → 95% of running words) is the strongest evidence for this learner. If a copy can be found through NZCER or a library, it is the closest thing to a ready-made Niuean core list. Two caveats apply: it reflects 1970s children's *writing*, and it is a spelling list, so it probably counts word forms, not lemmas.
- **Practical recipe for a ~1500-word list, in order of value:**
  1. Elley & Livingstone list, if obtainable.
  2. A frequency count of the 1983 Niuean Bible, which is the largest single public Niuean text. Its register is archaic and religious, so drop the Bible-specific vocabulary. It still ranks particles and core verbs well.
  3. Vocabulary from NZ curriculum and TKI resources (Ponataki, school wordlists) and from Vagahau Niue Week materials.
  4. A general core list (for example the English top 1000, or a Polynesian basic list) translated with Sperlich's dictionary and checked by a fluent family member.
- Words Niuean children used much more often than English lists predict (coconut, plantation, village, island) are a reminder to add cultural vocabulary by hand: kinship, food, church, fishing, umu, village names, and so on. That fits a learner whose goal is cultural connection.

## 2. What does coverage research say about the top 1000/1500/2000 words? Is "1500 words" a myth?

### Takeaway
"1500 words" is a reasonable **starting** target, not a fluency threshold. In English, the top 2,000 word families cover roughly 80% of written text and well above that in informal speech. Comfortable understanding needs about 95% coverage (some 2,000–3,000 families for listening) to 98% (6,000–9,000 families). Niuean is analytic and particle-heavy, so a small set of function words will cover an unusually large share of running speech. The 1970s Niuean data (900 words → 95%) is consistent with that.

### Cited Findings
- **Nation & Waring (1997).** In the Brown corpus, about 2,000 word families give "near to 80%" coverage of written text, so roughly one word in five is unknown. The same number of words covers informal spoken text much better: **about 96%** according to an older study (Schonell, Meddleton & Shaw, 1956). The chapter also cites **Laufer (1989)**, who found 95% coverage sufficient for "reasonable comprehension". — [Nation & Waring 1997 (Lextutor copy, snippet)](https://lextutor.ca/research/nation_waring_97.html); [Cambridge sample chapter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805215/84845/sample/9780521584845wsc00.pdf)
- **Nation (2006).** Reaching **98% coverage** takes **8,000–9,000 word families for written text** and **6,000–7,000 for spoken text**. By genre: novels need about 9,000 families, newspapers 8,000, children's movies 6,000 and spoken English 7,000, with proper nouns counted as known. — [Nation 2006 PDF](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf); [Schmitt et al. 2015 replication proposal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- **van Zeeland & Schmitt (2012).** **95% coverage may be enough for listening comprehension**, and that can be reached with **2,000–3,000 word families**. Schmitt, Cobb, Horst & Schmitt call Nation's 98% answer "rather daunting". — [Schmitt et al. 2015, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE); [Lextutor PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_cobb_etal_2015.pdf)
- **Adolphs & Schmitt (2003)**, "Lexical Coverage of Spoken Discourse", *Applied Linguistics* 24(4): 425–438. Based on the CANCODE corpus and the spoken BNC, it found that **more than 2,000 word families are needed** for everyday spoken English, which undercuts claims that 2,000 is enough. The two listings I found give the author order differently. — [Dialnet listing](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/metricas/documentos/ARTREV/752394)
- Coverage thresholds may be set too high for real-world use, because learners also lean on dictionaries and online support. — [Schmitt et al. 2015 (snippet)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE/core-reader)
- **Niuean-specific data point:** 900 words covered 95% of running words in Niuean children's writing. — [Commonwealth iLibrary (snippet)](https://thecommonwealth-ilibrary.org/index.php/comsec/catalog/download/247/244/1791?inline=1)

### Inferences
- **Myth versus reality.** Popular "learn 1500 words and you'll understand 80–90%" claims rest on research like the above, but they mix up *coverage* (the share of running words you recognise) with *comprehension*. At 80–90% coverage, one word in every five to ten is unknown, which usually means only partial understanding. 1500 words is a strong foundation: enough to follow familiar, contextual speech (family talk, church, greetings) and to start learning from context. It is not conversational fluency.
- **Why this suits Niuean.** Niuean is isolating: it has no verb inflection for tense or agreement ([Massam, via search snippet](https://core.ac.uk/download/481746913.pdf)). Grammar is carried by a small set of particles: TAM markers (*ne, kua, to*), case markers (*e, he, a, ke*), articles and number markers (*taha, tau*), and possessives. These are very frequent, so the top 100–200 items probably cover a larger share of text than in English, consistent with 900 words → 95% in children's writing.
- **Counting units differ.** English figures count *word families* (for example, *run* = runs/running/ran). Niuean has little inflection but uses reduplication, prefixes such as *faka-* (causative/manner) **[unverified]** and *toko-* (for counting people), and many compounds. In Niuean, 1500 headwords is roughly comparable to, or slightly more powerful than, 1500 English word families. Treat this as an estimate.
- **Feasibility over 180 days.** At 15–30 min/day, about 8–10 new words a day reaches ~1,500 headwords by day 150–180 with spaced repetition. A sensible priority order is: (1) the ~100 grammatical particles and pronouns, with the grammar in section 3, (2) the next ~900 content words, (3) heritage and cultural vocabulary. Passive exposure means much of the vocabulary may already be partly known, which speeds things up.

### Gaps
- No spoken-Niuean coverage study exists that I could find. The 1970s data covers written children's compositions only.
- **[unverified, not retrieved this session]** Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) reportedly put "minimal" comprehension at about 4,000–5,000 families (95%) and "optimal" at about 8,000 (98%). Confirm before citing.

## 3. Grammar essentials for a beginner

### Takeaway
Niuean is a verb-initial (VSO/VAO) language with ergative–absolutive case marking. Every clause starts with a preverbal tense/aspect particle (*ne* past, *kua* perfect, *to* future, *ha ne* progressive, zero for present/general). Noun phrases begin with case markers that differ for common nouns (*he* ergative, *e* absolutive) and for proper nouns and pronouns (*e* ergative, *a* absolutive). Pronouns distinguish singular, dual and plural, with inclusive and exclusive "we". A beginner who drills these particle "slots" can parse most sentences.

### Cited Findings
- **Word order and case.** Niuean is "a VSOX language, with an ergative case system which marks common nouns and proper nouns/pronouns differently". Ergative common = *he*, absolutive common = *e*; ergative proper = *e*, absolutive proper = *a*. Case markers are portmanteaus that also encode whether a noun is proper or common. — [Massam 2001, pseudo noun incorporation](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/rtk8/Massam%202001%20pseudo%20noun%20incorporation%20in%20Niuean%20clean%20copy.pdf); [Massam CLA 2006](https://cla-acl.artsci.utoronto.ca/pdfs/actes-2006/Massam.pdf)
- Because the absolutive is unmarked, transitive clauses often look like passives when translated literally. Some analysts prefer to call the order verb–agent–patient. — [Wikipedia mirror (snippet)](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/ISO_639:niu)
- **Example (Seiter 1980: 29):** *Ne kai he pusi ia e moa*, "That cat ate the chicken". Breakdown: past *ne* + verb *kai* ("eat") + ergative *he* + *pusi* ("cat") + *ia* ("that") + absolutive *e* + *moa* ("chicken"). The verb is usually preceded by a tense/aspect word. — [Ball, LFG 2004](https://typo.uni-konstanz.de/lfg-proceedings/LFGprocCSLI/LFG2004/pdfs/lfg04ball.pdf)
- Niuean also has **postverbal particles**, for example in a Seiter (1980: 69) sentence meaning "He's always fishing". — [Ball 2004](https://typo.uni-konstanz.de/lfg-proceedings/LFGprocCSLI/LFG2004/pdfs/lfg04ball.pdf)
- **TAM particles.** The TAM particle comes first, before the verb, optionally followed by negation and/or a modal, and it can be null. — [Massam CLA 2009](https://cla-acl.artsci.utoronto.ca/pdfs/actes-2009/CLA2009_Massam.pdf); [Massam, Lee & Rolle 2006](https://nzlingsoc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/massam-2006.pdf)
- A learner-level summary: present = null, past = *ne*, perfect = *kua*, future = *to*. — [UKLO problem handout (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/document/568886217/UKLO-R2-Q3)
- Massam's 2009 table also lists *ha ne* (progressive), but the extracted text was garbled. — [Massam 2009](https://cla-acl.artsci.utoronto.ca/pdfs/actes-2009/CLA2009_Massam.pdf)
- Niuean "lacks all tense and agreement inflection and nominative case". — [core.ac.uk thesis snippet](https://core.ac.uk/download/481746913.pdf)
- **Questions and negation.** *nakai* appears in Massam's examples glossed as a question marker, alongside perfect *kua*. — [Massam 2009](https://cla-acl.artsci.utoronto.ca/pdfs/actes-2009/CLA2009_Massam.pdf)
- Analyses of negation differ. Massam (2020) places a negative projection above TP. — [Middleton 2025, "Diagnosing negation in Polynesian"](https://repository.usp.ac.fj/id/eprint/15230/1/Middleton%202025%20Diagnosing%20negation%20in%20Polynesian.pdf)
- **Articles and number.** *taha* means "a, one" (singular indefinite) and *ha* is a "backgrounded" quantifier. Both appear without a linker. Possessors and numerals in quantifier position take a linker *a* or *e* (for example *falu a* "some", *loga e* "many", *gāhoa e* "few"). Number marking is null (singular) or *tau* (plural), as in *Ne kai e Sione e tau talo aki e huki*, "Sione ate the taros with a fork". — [Massam, Niuean NP (ICAL paper)](https://sil-philippines-languages.org/ical/papers/massam-niuean_NP.pdf); [Massam CLA 2006](https://cla-acl.artsci.utoronto.ca/pdfs/actes-2006/Massam.pdf)
- *tau* is the plural marker for both definite and indefinite NPs. — [Pollex, Sperlich 1997 entries](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/source/219/?page=24)
- **Pronouns.**
  - 1sg *au*.
  - Dual: *taua* ("we two", inclusive), *maua* ("we two", exclusive), *mua* ("you two"), *laua* ("they two").
  - Plural: *tautolu* ("we all", inclusive), *mautolu* ("we all", exclusive), *mutolu* ("you all"), *lautolu* ("they").
  - The dual and plural endings come from the numerals *ua* "two" and *tolu* "three". The paradigm table is attributed to Seiter (1980).
  - Sources: [Wiktionary: tautolu](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tautolu); [OpenEd Manitoba textbook (Seiter table)](https://pressbooks.openedmb.ca/wordandsentencestructures/?p=6637); [Wikipedia mirror](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/ISO_639:niu)
  - **[unverified]** The 2sg and 3sg forms are *koe* and *ia*.
- **Possessives.** *haaku* = "my, mine". *haau* and *ha/hoo* = 2sg "your". Possessives can come after the noun: *e tau mena gahua haau*, "your tools/work things". — [Pollex Sperlich entries](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/source/219/?page=24); [Massam NP paper](https://sil-philippines-languages.org/ical/papers/massam-niuean_NP.pdf)
- **Numerals.** *taha* 1, *ua* 2, *tolu* 3. 13 = *hogofulu ma tolu* ("ten and three"). 100 = *taha e teau*. Numerals take the prefix *toko-* when counting people. — [Omniglot: Niuean numbers](https://omniglot.com/language/numbers/niuean.htm); [Wikipedia mirror](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/ISO_639:niu)
  - **[unverified]** 4–10 are *fā, lima, ono, fitu, valu, hiva, hogofulu*.
- **Reference works.**
  - Seiter, W. (1980) *Studies in Niuean Syntax* (Garland). This is the classic syntax study, cited above via Ball 2004 and the OpenEd textbook.
  - Diane Massam's papers (University of Toronto): CLA proceedings 2006, 2009, 2012, and NZLS 2006.
  - A recent Niuean grammar monograph reviewed in Project MUSE. — [MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/806352) (title not visible in the snippet; **[unverified]** possibly Massam's 2020 OUP book *Niuean: Predicate-Initial Order and Clause Structure*)
  - Sperlich (ed.) 1997 *Tohi Vagahau Niue / Niue Language Dictionary*. — [Pollex](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/source/219/)
  - I found no source for "Kaulima's grammar".

### Inferences
- **A beginner sentence template:** `[TAM] [neg?] VERB [postverbal particle?] [he/e + agent] [e/a + patient] ...`.
  - Drill this as one "frame" with substitution, using the Seiter example as the model sentence.
  - Ergativity sounds intimidating, but in practice it amounts to "use *he* (or *e* for names) before the doer of a transitive verb, and *e* (or *a* for names) everywhere else in the core".
- Inclusive versus exclusive "we" (*taua/tautolu* versus *maua/mautolu*) matters socially. It is worth learning early for family and church settings, and it maps neatly onto the numerals for "two" and "three".
- The grammatical particles (TAM, case, *tau/taha/ha*, pronouns, possessives, *nakai*, linkers) are probably fewer than 100 items yet cover a large share of running text. They should come first in any frequency-based plan.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm with a source: the full semantics of *ha ne* and *to*, the full negation system (*nakai* as negator versus question particle), predicate *ko*, *ke* (subjunctive/purpose or oblique marker), the full possessive *a/o* class system (*haaku/hooku*), or postverbal particles like *agaia*, *tuai* and *foki*.

## 4. Orthography and pronunciation

### Takeaway
Niuean is written with a small Latin alphabet in which **g = /ŋ/ ("ng" as in *sing*)**. This is a deliberate choice: Niueans rejected an attempt to switch to "ng". Vowel length is phonemic and is marked with macrons in the modern standard, though usage varies. There is no glottal stop. There are two traditional dialects, Motu (north) and Tafiti (south), which differ mainly in vocabulary, and the differences have reportedly eroded.

### Cited Findings
- Niuean writes /ŋ/ as **g**, as Samoan does. McEwen's 1970 dictionary switched to *ng*, but Niueans objected strongly and the Niue Assembly passed a motion against it. The *g* is pronounced as in *sing*. — [MPP orthography guidelines (Niue)](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Resources/Orthography-guidelines/orthography-guidelines-niue.pdf); [Wikipedia (search snippet)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niuean_language)
- **Vowel length is phonemic.** The contemporary standard leaves short vowels unmarked and marks long vowels with a macron (or with double vowels). Some authorities do not promote macrons, so practice varies. — [MPP orthography guidelines](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Resources/Orthography-guidelines/orthography-guidelines-niue.pdf); [Rolle & Starks, "Vowel length in Niuean" (Oceanic Linguistics)](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/564185/); [Princeton listing](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/vowel-length-in-niuean/)
- **Two identical adjacent vowels** can form a "rearticulated" vowel, which is distinct from a single long vowel. — [search snippet: Rolle & Starks / JIPA Illustration](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/564185/)
- A *Journal of the International Phonetic Association* "Illustrations of the IPA: Niuean" exists with full phonetic detail. — [Cambridge JIPA: Niuean](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/product/3A2549DE96860C5485D96E7FE999D225/core-reader)
- An alphabet was codified in 1907. The modern orthographic choices are discussed in Sperlich's dictionary. — [snippets via MPP guidelines / Wikipedia](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Resources/Orthography-guidelines/orthography-guidelines-niue.pdf)
- **No glottal stop.** Niuean lost the glottal stop that Tongan keeps, which caused mergers. For example, Tongan *taʻu* ("year") and *tau* ("fight") both became Niuean *tau*. — [Wikipedia (snippet)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niuean_language)
- **Dialects.** The older **Motu** dialect is spoken in the north and **Tafiti** in the south. They differ mainly in vocabulary and slight pronunciation-based spelling variants, for example *volu* (Tafiti) versus *matā* (Motu) "to scrape", and *lala* (Tafiti) versus *kautoga* (Motu) "guava". One source says the differences have eroded. Another says the Motu/Tafiti dualism is less dramatic in Niuean culture than early writers (Smith) suggested. — [Wikipedia (snippet)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niuean_language); [ANU Pacific Linguistics PL-548](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstream/1885/254157/1/PL-548.295.pdf)

### Inferences
- The main beginner pitfalls are reading *g* as English /g/ (*vagahau* is "va-nga-hau") and ignoring vowel length, which changes meaning. Learning from audio (family members, RNZ Pacific Niuean news, Vagahau Niue Week media) rather than spelling alone helps, since macrons are inconsistent in printed materials.
- Because dialect differences are mostly lexical, the learner can ask family which village or region they come from and prefer those variants for heritage vocabulary.

### Gaps
- **[unverified]** The full letter inventory. My understanding is the vowels *a e i o u* plus consonants *f g h k l m n p s t v*, with *r* and *d* (and occasionally others) appearing in loanwords.
- **[unverified]** Stress rules. My understanding is penultimate-mora stress, as in most Polynesian languages; long vowels attract stress.

## 5. Relationship with Tongan, Samoan and Māori

### Takeaway
Niuean and Tongan form the **Tongic** subgroup of Polynesian, so Tongan is Niuean's closest relative. Samoan and Eastern Polynesian (including Māori) are more distant relatives that have influenced Niuean vocabulary and sounds. A learner with some exposure to Samoan, Tongan or te reo Māori will find many recognisable cognates.

### Cited Findings
- Niuean is most closely related to Tongan and slightly more distantly to Māori, Samoan and Hawaiian. Tongan and Niuean form the Tongic subgroup, possibly with Niuafoʻouan. — [Wikipedia: Niuean (snippet)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niuean_language); [Wikipedia: Tongic languages](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongic_languages)
- Niuean shows Samoan and Eastern Polynesian influence. One article finds phonological and lexical features that it takes as evidence of Eastern Polynesian contact. — [Oceanic Linguistics article (MUSE)](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/209361); [Wikipedia (snippet)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niuean_language)
- Pollex-Online lists Niuean reflexes of Proto-Polynesian forms. For example, *maua* (we two, exclusive) comes from Proto-Polynesian *(ki)ma(a)ua*. Pollex can be used to look up shared roots across Tongan, Samoan and Māori. — [Pollex: MAA-UA](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/entry/maa-ua); [Pollex Niue language index](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/language/niue/?page=31)

### Inferences
- Many core words are widely shared across Polynesian, for example *ika* (fish), *fale* (house), *moana* (sea), *tolu* (three), *lima* (five/hand) **[unverified as a group, though *tolu* is cited above]**. A heritage learner who hears Samoan or Māori in New Zealand can use these as memory anchors. Watch for false friends and for sound correspondences (Niuean *h* often matching Samoan *s* or Māori *h* **[unverified]**).

## 6. Language status: speakers, endangerment, census

### Takeaway
Niuean is endangered. UNESCO rates it as "definitely endangered", though some sources say "severely". On Niue (about 1,500–1,700 residents) a majority still speak it: 72% self-reported proficiency in 2017, down from 86% in 2006. Most Niueans live in New Zealand (about 35,000 by ethnicity in 2023), where only about 4,000 speak the language, mostly in Auckland. Fluency in New Zealand fell from 32% (1996) to about 20% (2018), and other sources give about 12%. A heritage learner is joining an active revitalisation effort.

### Cited Findings
- **UNESCO status.** Most sources say "definitely endangered", including a 2023 University of Auckland resource and a 2018 RNZ report. Waatea News (2022) and a Tāoga Niue paper say "severely endangered", the latter citing a 2005 assessment. A 2025 RNZ piece says only "endangered". — [UoA niuean-2023.pdf](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/assets/science/for/maori-and-pacific-students/pacific-languages/niuean-2023.pdf); [RNZ 2018](https://www.rnz.co.nz/international/pacific-news/368698/push-to-support-endangered-niuean-language); [Waatea News 2022](https://waateanews.com/?p=456702); [Tāoga Niue, "Is Vagahau Niue an endangered language?"](https://taoganiue.nu/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Is-Vagahau-Niue-Endangered.pdf); [RNZ Oct 2025](https://www.rnz.co.nz/international/pacific-news/576867/beautiful-language-advocate-champions-revival-of-vagahau-niue)

### Inferences
- The New Zealand diaspora is about 20 times the island population, but only about 10–12% of New Zealand Niueans speak the language, and most of them are older. Recruiting a fluent relative (often a grandparent) as a conversation partner is probably the single highest-value resource for this learner. Auckland, home to 85% of New Zealand speakers, is where community classes and church services in Niuean are concentrated.
- The 2018 and 2023 New Zealand speaker counts (4,182 and about 4,035) suggest roughly flat or slightly declining absolute numbers while the ethnic population grew from about 30,900 to about 34,900, so the speaker share is shrinking.

### Gaps
- **Australia.** I found no source for Niuean speaker numbers in Australia. The ABS census would be the place to check.
- The 2023 New Zealand speaker count is ambiguous (4,035 versus 3,435) and should be checked against the Stats NZ Aotearoa Data Explorer.
- Language tables from Niue's 2022 census were not retrieved.
