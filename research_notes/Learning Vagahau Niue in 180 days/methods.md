# Evidence-based methods for an adult heritage learner of Vagahau Niue (180 days, 15–30 min/day)

> Method note: page fetches were blocked in this session for some hosts (rnz.co.nz, fpcc.ca returned DNS failures), so most findings below come from search-result abstracts and snippets of the cited pages rather than full-text reading. Where a claim rests on a secondary or anecdotal source, it is flagged. Evidence strength labels: **[Strong]** = peer-reviewed experimental/meta-analytic; **[Moderate]** = peer-reviewed qualitative/observational or official guidance; **[Weak/Popular]** = practitioner claims, blogs, forum anecdotes.

## 1. Heritage learner research: strengths, gaps, emotions, what helps (incl. Pacific/Māori context and Niuean status)

### Takeaway
Heritage speakers typically keep an advantage in pronunciation/sound system and receptive skills, while production and some grammar areas are weaker in patterned (not random) ways; low-proficiency "overhearers" behave much like L2 learners linguistically but bring identity and relationships, which Māori research shows are decisive. Vagahau Niue is UNESCO "definitely endangered" with a small, aging speaker base, so each family speaker is a scarce, high-value resource.

### Cited Findings
- Early home exposure usually leaves imbalanced bilingualism favouring the dominant language, but some heritage-language abilities persist; choosing the right baseline is a known methodological challenge. **[Moderate]** — [Polinsky 2015 overview (Harvard DASH)](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/7312037d-e57a-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/download)
- Polinsky ("What they know well"): heritage speakers keep a lasting edge over L2 learners in some areas; their sound production stays close to monolingual controls, and their divergences from baseline are patterned and obey general natural-language constraints. **[Moderate]** — [Polinsky abstract, Univ. Potsdam](https://www.uni-potsdam.de/fileadmin01/projects/slavistik-slavische-sprachwissenschaft/Polinsky_Abstract.pdf)
- Changes in heritage grammars are restrictive and hit some grammatical domains harder than others (review of Polinsky 2018, *Heritage Languages and Their Speakers*). **[Moderate]** — [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/751040)
- Montrul (2008) review: most heritage speakers raised/schooled in the host country show noticeable gaps; distinguishes "heritage speakers" from "heritage language learners." **[Moderate]** — [Montrul 2008, Second Language Research (HAL preprint)](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00570753/file/PEER_stage2_10.1177%252F0267658308095738.pdf)
- Narrow definitions of heritage speaker require real proficiency from childhood; Polinsky argues heritage learners lacking that proficiency are linguistically much like other L2 learners. **[Moderate]** — [Harvard DASH, Polinsky Heritage](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/3382973/2/Polinsky_Heritage.pdf); [Annual Review of Linguistics](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-030521-050236)
- NZ-born Pasifika young adults (18–25) express intense apprehension about diminishing heritage-language ability, linked to identity security (Samu 2019). **[Moderate, qualitative]** — [Sydney Open Journals (IEJ)](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IEJ/article/view/14038/12639)
- Recurrent identity-policing debate in the Sāmoan diaspora over whether one is "real" without the language (opinion piece, captures roots of language shame). **[Weak/opinion]** — [University of Auckland News 2022](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2022/06/01/haunting-issues-for-samoan-diaspora.html)
- Rātima & May (2011) identify ten factors that help/hinder adult Māori L2 proficiency: aptitude, age, attitudes/motivation, learning strategies, instruction, **agency and anxiety**, **wairua (spiritual dimension)**, demography, language status, language planning. **[Moderate, review]** — [MAI Review PDF](https://journal.mai.ac.nz/system/files/maireview/412-3077-1-PB.pdf)
- Rātima (2013 PhD, 17 highly proficient adult L2 te reo speakers): the ability to establish and maintain **loving, supportive relationships with Māori-language speakers** was crucial; mainstream SLA motivation theories do not capture Māori experiences. **[Moderate, qualitative]** — [Waikato Research Commons](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/handle/10289/7396); [Ako Aotearoa summary](https://ako.ac.nz/knowledge-centre/the-development-of-proficiency-in-te-reo-maori-with-adult-learners)
- Vagahau Niue is classified "Definitely Endangered" in the UNESCO Atlas; reported figures include fewer than ~7,000 fluent speakers worldwide (mostly elderly) and under 11% of Niueans in Aotearoa able to speak it (figures vary by source). **[Moderate, aggregated reporting]** — [AUT News](https://www.aut.ac.nz/news/stories/hope-for-the-niuean-language-in-aotearoa); [MPP: Loss of Vagahau Niue a real concern](https://mpia.govt.nz/latest-news/loss-of-vagahau-niue-a-real-concern/); [Te Ao News 2023](https://teaonews.co.nz/2023/05/12/revitalisation-efforts-continue-for-endangered-niuean-language); [Inside Government 2026: "Niue language vulnerable, says minister"](https://insidegovernment.co.nz/niue-language-vulnerable-says-minister/)
- NZ Ministry for Pacific Peoples' Pacific Languages Strategy 2022–2032 (with a Niuean-language version "Ko e Taue he Vagahau Niue") is the main policy frame; MPP's Niue research found community concern about decline and called for education support. **[Moderate, official]** — [Pacific Languages Strategy PDF](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Pacific-Languages-Strategy/Pacific-Languages-Strategy-2022-2032.pdf); [Niuean version](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Pacific-Languages-Strategy/PLS-Ko-E-Taue-He-Vagahau-Niue-2022-2032-web.pdf); [Leo Moana Niue report](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Reports/Leo-Moana/0072-Leo-Moana-Ethnic-Specific-Report-NIUE_-8_FINAL.pdf)
- AUT research examines receptive bilingualism among first-generation NZ-born Niue and church-based revitalisation in Auckland — directly relevant to a learner who "understands a little but can't speak." **[Moderate, thesis]** — [AUT open repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/bitstreams/5f67f149-9a02-4787-a95c-bd149cad3fb1/download); [AUT repository record](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/11238?show=full)
- Official orthography guidelines for Vagahau Niue exist (MPP), useful for consistent spelling in personal flashcards. — [MPP Orthography guidelines: Niue](https://mpia.govt.nz/assets/Resources/Orthography-guidelines/orthography-guidelines-niue.pdf)
- Learner resources: free app *Fakaako e Vagahau Niue* (Vagahau Niue Trust & Takatakai Moana, launched July 2022; words, alphabet, conversations, numbers, proverbs, tap-to-hear audio, record-your-own narration) — [MPP: Tool aims to sustain Vagahau Niue](https://mpia.govt.nz/latest-news/tool-aims-to-sustain-vagahau-niue/); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/sr/app/fakaako-e-vagahau-niue/id1587065389); [Scoop 2022](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/BU2207/S00135.htm). Also: AUT's online dictionary Tohi Vagahau Niue, Ponataki resource (2016), NZ Curriculum teaching materials, NZQA NCEA vocabulary lists — [TKI Vagahau Niue](https://pasifika.tki.org.nz/Pacific-languages/Vagahau-Niue/(tab)/1733); [Ponataki PDF](https://pasifika.tki.org.nz/content/download/2963/21181/file/Ponataki+-+Niue+Language+Educational+Resource.pdf); [NZ Curriculum: Teaching and learning vagahau Niue](https://newzealandcurriculum.tahurangi.education.govt.nz/5637167099.p); [NZQA Vagahau Niue](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/subjects/subject/vagahau-niue); [Omniglot](https://omniglot.com/writing/niuean.php). Niue Language Week (Faahi Tapu he Vagahau Niue, October) is an annual focal event — [University of Auckland 2024](https://auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2024/10/13/celebrate-faahi-tapu-he-vagahau-niue-niue-language-week.html)

### Inferences
- For this learner, expect fast gains in listening and good pronunciation; budget most effort for **speaking production** and high-frequency grammar patterns (e.g., sentence frames, particles), which is where heritage gaps cluster.
- Because relationships with speakers were the decisive factor for proficient Māori adult learners, the plan should treat family sessions as the core, not an add-on, and protect relationships (low-pressure, warm, no correcting-in-public).
- Language shame/anxiety is plausible and documented in Pacific diaspora identity discourse; build in explicit "permission to make mistakes" agreements with family and a wairua/purpose element (hymns, prayers, family stories) to sustain motivation.
- A Niuean milestone aligned with Niue Language Week (October) could serve as a public, motivating checkpoint if the 180 days overlap.

### Gaps
- No study found that specifically measures language shame/anxiety among Niuean or other Pacific heritage learners in NZ/Australia; Horwitz's anxiety work appears only in citations.
- Valdés' heritage-learner pedagogy was not retrieved in this session.
- Hawaiian adult learner outcome research was not located.
- Current (late 2026) availability of learnniue.com, Tohi Vagahau Niue and the app was not verified.
- RNZ Oct 2025 profile of a Vagahau Niue advocate could not be fetched ([URL](https://www.rnz.co.nz/international/pacific-news/576867/beautiful-language-advocate-champions-revival-of-vagahau-niue)).

## 2. Master-Apprentice (Hinton), "Where Are Your Keys?", Te Ataarangi/Māori adult approaches — what transfers to short daily family sessions

### Takeaway
MAP (one-to-one, target-language-only, everyday activities, apprentice-led, culture with language) has positive but qualitative evidence for adults; standard programs assume hundreds of hours a year, so 15–30 min/day is a "micro-MAP." WAYK and Te Ataarangi contribute concrete, gesture/object-based techniques that make short sessions with non-teacher relatives productive.

### Cited Findings
- MAP was developed by Leanne Hinton with the Advocates for Indigenous California Language Survival (early 1990s) for Karuk, Hupa, Yurok, Wintu, Yowlumne, Mojave; designed so communities without linguistic training can run it, matching one-to-one, voluntary learning styles. **[Moderate]** — [Wikipedia: MAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master-Apprentice_Language_Learning_Program)
- Core principles (FPCC "10 Points," adapted from Hinton 2002 *How to Keep Your Language Alive*): use only the target language; language as everyday life, not a lesson subject; teach language and culture together; the apprentice actively steers learning; patience and sensitivity; prioritise speaking/listening over writing/grammar analysis; use recordings. **[Moderate, practitioner guidance]** — [FPCC 10 Points PDF](https://sandbox.fpcc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/TenPointsForLanguageLearning.pdf) (content via search snippet; fetch blocked); [older version](https://sandbox.fpcc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/FPCC_MAP_10PointsForLanguageLearning.pdf)
- McIvor & Jacobs (UVic) 3-year study, 33 adult learners + mentors, 111 interviews in BC: MAP positively impacted learning and wellbeing/worthiness; challenges were learner **exhaustion** (full-time workers/caregivers) and discouragement from lack of domains of use. **[Moderate, mixed/qualitative]** — [UVic News 2016](https://www.uvic.ca/news/topics/2016+indigenous-language-revitalization-adult-learners-study+ring)
- ~90% of apprentices reported improved speaking; participants asked to expand the current 300 hours/year support to 1,000 hours/year for 3–5 years. **[Moderate, self-report]** — [NETOLNEW summary report](https://netolnew.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/NETOLNEW-summary-report-UVic-screen.pdf); [UVic dspace](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/bitstreams/0dc040b1-ea95-44d2-a132-4fd4aa5ee437/download)
- Program hour norms: Manitoba recommends ~900 immersion hours over 3 years (~10 hrs/week); NWT pays for 200 program hours. — [Smithsonian Folklife: Manitoba MAP](https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/stream-of-voices-master-apprentice-language-learning-program-manitoba); [Wikipedia MAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master-Apprentice_Language_Learning_Program)
- Outcomes chapter: McIvor, Jacobs & Jenni (2023) "Reviving languages: Outcomes of a Mentor-Apprentice style learning study" (De Gruyter). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515%2F9783110712742-032) (not read)
- "Where Are Your Keys?" (Evan Gardner): game-based, uses gesture/sign to get immediate communication in the target language, partly based on Total Physical Response; usable with any language that has a willing fluent speaker; techniques include "copy-cat" (imitating a fluent player early and often), "fluency = flow," "my turn/your turn," "start over," "just in time." **[Weak/Popular – practitioner; little controlled evidence]** — [Wikipedia: WAYK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where_Are_Your_Keys%3F); [WAYK technique: fluency](https://whereareyourkeys.org/tag/technique-fluency); [WAYK: my turn/your turn](https://whereareyourkeys.org/tag/technique-my-turnyour-turn); [Gardner & Ciotti 2018 chapter (Berkeley)](https://lx.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/gardner_ciotti_2018_wayk.pdf); [CBC explainer](https://www.cbc.ca/news/how-the-where-are-your-keys-language-teaching-method-works-1.4663929)
- Te Ataarangi: community-based te reo programme using Cuisenaire rods (rākau) and spoken language only; developed by Kāterina Mataira and Ngoi Pēwhairangi in the late 1970s (Tokomaru Bay); has taught thousands of adults. **[Moderate, descriptive]** — [Te Ara: the rākau method](https://teara.govt.nz/en/photograph/41083/the-rakau-method); [RNZ: two kuia teaching te reo](https://static1.radionz.co.nz/national/programmes/teahikaa/audio/201818069/two-kuia-with-a-passion-for-teaching-te-reo); [Waikato Independent 2017](https://waikatoindependent.co.nz/2017/05/te-reo-maori-good-start/30401)
- Learner-centred approaches to adult te reo L2 (Otago PhD, 10 learners, 5 teachers). **[Moderate, qualitative]** — [Birnie 2017 PhD](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/bitstream/handle/10523/7981/BirnieJohnD2017PhD.pdf)
- Ministry of Education report on Māori adult learners notes paucity of research on adult learner voices. — [Education Counts](https://www.educationcounts.govt.nz/publications/80898/hangaia-te-matapuna-o-te-mohia)

### Inferences
- Transfer to 15–30 min/day: run 2–4 "micro-immersion" family sessions per week (in person or video/phone) around real activities (cooking, church, greeting, family photos), apprentice brings the agenda ("today I want to learn how to say grace / ask about your village"), speaker stays in Niuean, learner uses gestures and set "survival phrases" (e.g., "How do you say…?", "Again, slowly please") taught first.
- WAYK/Te Ataarangi-style object games are useful because relatives need no teaching skill; a fixed object set (cup, food, photos) plus "my turn/your turn" provides structure in 10–15 minutes.
- MAP's exhaustion finding argues for short, sustainable sessions rather than ambitious ones; 180 days × ~20 min ≈ 60 hours, far below MAP norms (200–900+ hours), so expectations must be scaled.

### Gaps
- No published evaluation found for "Mauri Ora" or "language champion" programmes, or for Kura Reo outcomes.
- No controlled study of WAYK effectiveness found.
- No documented Niuean MAP adaptation found.

## 3. Vocabulary acquisition science: SRS/FSRS, retrieval, new words/day, context vs isolation, chunks

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice and spacing are the strongest-evidence tools; with FSRS, new-card count is purely a workload choice (≈10 reviews/day per daily new card is Anki's rule of thumb), so 5–10 new cards/day fits 15–30 minutes. Chunks/formulaic phrases matter for fluency and are a natural fit for prayers, greetings and protocol, though teaching-method evidence is thinner.

### Cited Findings
- Karpicke & Roediger 2008 (*Science* 319:966–968), Swahili–English vocabulary: repeated studying after learning had no effect on delayed recall, repeated testing produced a large positive effect; students' predictions were uncorrelated with performance. **[Strong]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18276894/); replication: [Soderstrom et al.](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/soderstorm_kerr.pdf)
- Anki manual rule of thumb: learning 20 new cards/day consistently ⇒ roughly 200 reviews/day. **[Moderate, official docs]** — [Anki deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)
- FSRS works equally well whether learning 5 or 50 new cards/day; new-card volume is a workload decision. — [Anki FSRS FAQ](https://faqs.ankiweb.net/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs.html)
- Higher desired retention increases workload; very low retention increases relearning; 0.90 is a common starting point; Anki includes an FSRS simulator to preview workload (expressed in time). — [FSRS wiki: optimal retention](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention); [Anki forum: approximate workload](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/questions-about-approximate-workload-when-changing-desired-retention/63860.md)
- Anecdote: raising retention 90%→95% in a 17,000-card deck took reviews from ~150 to 4,000+/day. **[Weak/anecdote]** — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/i-raised-fsrs-desired-ret-and-reviews-shot-up-radically/55628.md)
- Nation: word-card (flashcard) learning typically produces close to 100% learning of target items; deliberate/flash-card learning needs fewer repetitions than incidental learning (~10–12 repetitions for many words incidentally); rich classroom instruction ≈3–5 min/word. **[Moderate, expert synthesis]** — [Nation, Deliberate vocabulary learning from word cards (Cambridge)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/learning-vocabulary-in-another-language/deliberate-vocabulary-learning-from-word-cards/74EB1B4830C8A6C6A9E1490BCA444AD3); [Nation, How vocabulary is learned](https://media.neliti.com/media/publications/242518-how-vocabulary-is-learned-45a2c109.pdf); [Nation 2021 (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/document/708092927/Nation-2021)
- Long-term rates: Nation's input-based estimates allow ~1,000 word families/year; Webb & Nation reportedly suggest ~400 families/year for a dedicated classroom learner (secondhand). **[Moderate / Weak secondhand]** — [Nation, input for 9,000 words (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/323110087.pdf); [Goodreads blog summary](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/24646681-how-to-learn-vocabulary-in-another-language)
- Lexical Approach (Lewis) and chunks: Wray listed 50+ terms; "formulaic language" now the umbrella term; eye-tracking evidence (Ellis et al. 2008) suggests chunks are processed holistically. **[Moderate, review]** — [Cambridge Papers in ELT: Chunks (Thornbury 2019)](https://www.cambridge.org/gb/files/3815/6941/9472/CambridgePapersInELT_Chunks_2019_ONLINE.pdf); [Cambridge English: Learning language in chunks](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/765399-learning-language-in-chunks.pdf)
- Learners gain much from a sizable repertoire of formulaic sequences but are slow to close the gap with natives (Boers & colleagues review). **[Moderate]** — [Annual Review of Applied Linguistics: experimental & intervention studies on formulaic sequences](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/experimental-and-intervention-studies-on-formulaic-sequences-in-a-second-language/A2ACDF54604CFAC4443240748360C403)
- L2 learners can derive and use chunks but not on par with L1 speakers (Wang & Christiansen 2024 review). — [ERIC EJ1457275](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1457275.pdf); Wray 2013 timeline: treating language as single words + rules makes formulaic language hard — [Wray 2013 (Cardiff ORCA)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/44815/1/Wray%202013.pdf)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic: 5 new cards/day × 180 days = 900; 10/day = 1,800. Using Anki's ~10:1 heuristic, 5 new/day ⇒ ~50 reviews/day (~8–12 min); 10 new/day ⇒ ~100 reviews/day (~15–20 min) at steady state. For a 15–30 min budget that must also include listening and speaking, **5–7 new cards/day (≈900–1,250 items) is realistic; 10/day only on 30-minute days.** (Review-time-per-card is an assumption, not sourced.)
- Make cards sentence/chunk-based with family audio (audio front → meaning/production back), mined from things said by relatives, songs and the app; production cards (English/picture → say it aloud in Niuean) target the heritage production gap.
- Use desired retention ~0.85–0.90; avoid raising it — workload balloons.
- Prioritise high-frequency function words and formulas (greetings like "Fakaalofa lahi atu," thanks, prayer/grace frames, "How do you say…").

### Gaps
- No frequency list / corpus-based core vocabulary for Vagahau Niue was found; NZQA NCEA lists and the app are the best proxies.
- No direct evidence comparing context vs isolation retrieved this session (Nation's word-card guidelines list not obtained).
- Exact seconds-per-review figures for FSRS not sourced.

## 4. Comprehensible input, output/interaction, shadowing, songs/hymns

### Takeaway
Input is necessary but not sufficient; interaction with negotiation of meaning has meta-analytic support, which favours family conversation practice. Singing measurably improves verbatim recall of foreign phrases (one solid experiment), supporting hymns/songs; shadowing is popular but evidence is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Krashen: acquisition occurs by understanding messages (comprehensible input); critics argue input alone is insufficient and he neglected interaction and output. **[Moderate, theoretical debate]** — [ERIC ED507194](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED507194.pdf)
- Long's interaction hypothesis (1981, revised 1996): negotiation of meaning facilitates acquisition. — [Gass & Mackey excerpt, Cambridge](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811084/99637/excerpt/9781108499637_excerpt.pdf)
- Mackey & Goo meta-analysis (28 interaction studies): large effect sizes for learners engaged in interaction vs. not. **[Strong]** — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/78A156EE200F744F5978F99BFB073DBE); [Language Teaching 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444818000125)
- Ludke, Ferreira & Overy 2014 (*Memory & Cognition* 42:41–52): 60 adults learning Hungarian phrases by listen-and-repeat; the singing condition outperformed speaking and rhythmic speaking, significantly on recall/production tests; not explained by musical training, age, mood. **[Strong, single RCT]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23860945/); [PDF](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Ludke-et-al.-2014_SingingForeignLanguage_Mem-Cog.pdf); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/713232)
- Shadowing: narrative review (Hamada 2018, RELC Journal) recommends beginners start with shadowing for listening then progress to speaking; a 2024 systematic review (student dissertation, 44 studies) found over-reliance on controlled tasks; a 2024 primary study found non-significant oral proficiency differences. No meta-analysis found. **[Weak–Moderate]** — [RELC Journal 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380); [Oxford ORA dissertation](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2); [Dergipark 2024 study](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3392195)
- Church-based revitalisation is documented in the Auckland Niue community (hymns/church as a language domain). — [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/11238?show=full)

### Inferences
- Daily listening to family recordings, hymns and the app's narrated content provides input; family conversation provides the interaction that has the strongest evidence.
- Learning hymns, prayers and songs by listen-and-sing is evidence-backed for verbatim memory of fixed texts — ideal for protocol pieces (Lord's Prayer, grace, a hymn, a short mihi-equivalent introduction) — but verbatim recall is not the same as generative speaking; pair with chunk extraction (pull reusable phrases out of songs).
- Shadowing family audio is a low-risk pronunciation/fluency drill but should not be oversold.

### Gaps
- Swain's output hypothesis primary source not retrieved.
- No studies found on music-based learning in Polynesian languages specifically.

## 5. Realistic outcomes in ~45–90 hours; habit formation and adherence

### Takeaway
45–90 hours is roughly one-quarter to one-half of the ~180–200 guided hours Cambridge cites for A2 (from zero), so a true beginner would land around A1; a heritage learner with receptive background can plausibly reach strong A1/low A2 speaking with higher listening, plus a memorised repertoire of cultural texts. Habit automaticity takes a median ~66 days (range 18–254), and occasional misses don't derail it.

### Cited Findings
- Cambridge guided learning hours (cumulative, from beginner): A2 Key ≈180–200; B1 Preliminary ≈350–400; B2 First ≈500–600; A1 ≈90–100 (secondary). **[Moderate, reproduced official table]** — [LTTC/NTU table](https://www.lttc.ntu.edu.tw/en/MSmain_About_Test); [open-exam-prep (secondary)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cambridge-a2-key/introduction/study-hours-path)
- Another secondary table: A1 90–100, A2 190–250, B1 350–490, B2 530–750 hours incl. self-study. **[Weak/secondary]** — [QuillBot blog](https://quillbot.com/blog/language-learning/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language/)
- Cambridge ELT paper cautions that "basic fluency in X hours" claims are uninterpretable without a defined level. — [Cambridge ELT: How long does it take…](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/How-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-foreign-language.pdf)
- FSI: Category I 600–750 class hours, Category II ~900, III ~1,100, IV 2,200 to professional working proficiency, assuming strong aptitude, full-time intensive classes. Niuean is not on the FSI list. **[Moderate, official]** — [State Dept archived FSI page](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20210105072258/https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training)
- Lally et al. 2010 (*EJSP*, DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674): 96 participants, daily behaviour for 84 days; time to plateau automaticity averaged 66 days, range 18 to a predicted 254; early repetitions give the biggest automaticity gains; missing one opportunity didn't materially affect habit formation; very inconsistent performers failed; "21 days" is a myth. **[Strong, single study]** — [UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [UCL BSH blog](https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/bsh/2012/06/29/busting-the-21-days-habit-formation-myth/.); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-long-does-it-really-take-to-form-a-habit/)
- Adult MAP learners struggle with exhaustion from work/caregiving and lack of places to use the language. — [UVic News](https://www.uvic.ca/news/topics/2016+indigenous-language-revitalization-adult-learners-study+ring)

### Inferences
- Realistic 180-day targets (≈45 h at 15 min/day; ≈90 h at 30 min/day): understand most slow, familiar family talk; hold short, scaffolded everyday conversations (greetings, family, food, church, plans); perform from memory 1–3 hymns/songs, a prayer/grace, and a short self-introduction/genealogy speech; ~900–1,250 known words/chunks receptively, fewer productively. Calling this "CEFR A2" would be optimistic for speaking; "A1+/A2 listening" is defensible.
- Habit design: anchor sessions to an existing daily cue (same time/place), keep a "minimum viable day" (5 min of reviews) so misses are rare; expect the routine to feel automatic around weeks 9–10 at the earliest — the first ~2 months are the fragile phase, so phase 1 should be the simplest.

### Gaps
- No Niuean-specific proficiency framework or hour benchmarks found; NCEA levels could be a proxy but weren't mapped.
- No evidence on heritage-learner "head start" quantified in hours.

## 6. Recording and documenting family speakers: ethics, consent, protocol, personal audio decks

### Takeaway
Language-documentation ethics stresses informed, explicit consent (including about where recordings go), sensitivity around sacred/private content and community-set protocols; free templates and checklists exist. Family recording is less formally covered, but the same principles scale down.

### Cited Findings
- Informed consent: participants give explicit consent after being informed; sensitive content (politics, relationships) needs extra care (Austin, ethics chapter). **[Moderate]** — [Univ. Melbourne Minerva](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/41bfb743-2a1c-4de2-b3ba-c326bb9cb1bd/content)
- Consent for archiving/online publication is complex; both assuming consent and withholding publication can be paternalistic. — [NSF PAR paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10024418)
- Beginner toolkit (UAlberta/UNESCO) for documenting stories told by relatives includes a consent form, verbal consent script and recording checklist. **[Moderate, practical]** — [UAlberta ERA toolkit](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/a42d79df-b46f-4dc5-9eba-d4608de555a6/download/bfa5d18d-557a-47a0-84b5-7ad9f85a79ec)
- FPCC sample documents: protocol agreements can state that certain language/cultural teachings are sacred and not to be documented; consent templates for recording elders/fluent speakers; points to the Australian Guide to Community Protocols for Indigenous Language Projects. — [FPCC sample documents](https://fpcc.ca/resource/language-revitalization-research-projects-sample-documents/)
- AILDI (Arizona) checklist and handout for community documentation. — [AILDI checklist](https://www.aildi.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/data/Checklist.pdf); [AILDI handout](https://aildi.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/data/Handout.pdf)
- MAP guidance recommends recordings to support the apprentice. — [Wikipedia MAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master-Apprentice_Language_Learning_Program)
- The *Fakaako e Vagahau Niue* app supports recording your own narration. — [MPP](https://mpia.govt.nz/latest-news/tool-aims-to-sustain-vagahau-niue/)

### Inferences
- Practical protocol for the plan: ask each relative (verbally, recorded) what can be recorded, who may hear it (just me / family / public), whether it may be put in an app/deck, and whether anything (certain prayers, genealogies, church items) should not be recorded; re-confirm before sharing beyond personal use; give copies back to the family; label files with speaker, date, consent level.
- Personal audio decks: short clips (one sentence/chunk each) cut from recordings, imported into Anki with transcription checked against the orthography guidelines; this doubles as family heritage preservation.

### Gaps
- No Niue-specific or Pacific-specific cultural protocol guidance for recording elders was found (e.g., from the Vagahau Niue Trust); the learner should ask family/church elders directly.
- Australian Guide to Community Protocols for Indigenous Language Projects not retrieved.
- Legal aspects (privacy law in Australia/NZ for family recordings) not researched.
